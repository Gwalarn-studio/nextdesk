50 ATS Resume Keywords That Get Past the Software

The words recruiters search for, how to pick the right ones from a job ad, and where to put them so both software and humans read them.

Updated October 23, 2026
In short
  • An ATS stores your resume as text. Recruiters then search and filter that text, so the exact words matter.
  • The best keywords come from the job ad itself. The 50 below are a starting list, not a magic formula.
  • Put keywords in your headline, summary, skills section and experience bullets, always with proof.
  • Avoid keyword stuffing, hidden white text, and layouts with images or complex tables.

How ATS keyword matching works, in plain words

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software many companies use to receive and organize job applications. When you upload your resume, the system reads it and turns it into plain text. It then tries to fill fields such as name, job titles, dates, education and skills.

After that, a recruiter usually works with a list of candidates. They can search it, filter it, and sort it. Some systems also show a match score that compares your resume with the job description. Others rely on screening questions, like "Do you have the right to work in this country?"

So the real goal is simple: when a recruiter searches for a skill, your resume should show up. That only happens if the words they type are in your resume, spelled the way they expect.

You may have read that most resumes are "rejected by robots" before a human sees them. That claim is repeated often, but it is not well sourced, and it depends a lot on the company and the software. A more useful way to think about it: the ATS does not hate you. It just cannot guess what you did not write.

How to pick keywords from a job ad

Generic keyword lists help you think. But the job ad you are answering is the best source. Follow these steps for each application.

  1. Copy the full ad into a document. Include the title, the requirements and the "nice to have" part.
  2. Highlight the hard skills. These are tools, methods, certifications and domains: "SQL", "Salesforce", "budget management".
  3. Highlight the soft skills and work style. For example: "stakeholder management", "asynchronous communication", "self-starter".
  4. Note what is repeated. A skill that appears two or three times is probably important to the hiring team.
  5. Write down the exact wording. If the ad says "customer success", do not write only "client care". Use their term.
  6. Keep only what is true for you. Cross out anything you cannot back up in an interview.
  7. Match each keyword to a proof. A project, a result, a tool you used every day. This proof becomes a bullet point.
Tip: Write both the full term and the short form once, for example "search engine optimization (SEO)". Recruiters search for either one.

Where to place your keywords

Placement matters for two reasons. Some systems give more weight to certain sections. And a recruiter scanning your resume reads the top first.

  • Headline or target title. Under your name, use the job title from the ad if it honestly fits you. "Customer Success Manager" is easier to find than "Client Happiness Hero".
  • Summary. Three or four lines with your main skills and one result. This is where your top 3-5 keywords go.
  • Skills section. A simple list of tools and hard skills. Keep it short and relevant to the role.
  • Experience bullets. The most important place. Here each keyword is backed by a verb and a result.
  • Your LinkedIn profile. Recruiters search there too. Use the same keywords in your headline and "About" section. Our LinkedIn profile checklist walks you through it.

Use standard section titles: "Experience", "Education", "Skills". Creative titles can confuse the parser.

Common mistakes that hide a good resume

Keyword stuffing

Repeating "project management" fifteen times or pasting the whole job ad into your resume might raise a score. But a human reads your resume next, and stuffing looks like a trick. Use each important keyword where it is true and natural, usually once or twice.

White text and hidden keywords

Some people hide keywords in white text. Don't. The ATS turns your resume into plain text, so the hidden words often become visible to the recruiter. It can cost you the job for looking dishonest.

Images, icons and complex layouts

Text inside an image is often not read at all. Skill bars, logos and icons carry no words. Multi-column layouts and complex tables can be read in the wrong order, mixing dates and job titles. A clean single-column layout is the safest choice.

Contact details in the header or footer

Some parsers skip headers and footers. Put your name, email and phone in the main body, at the top.

Only abbreviations, or only full terms

Write "customer relationship management (CRM)" once, then "CRM". You cover both searches.

The wrong file type

Follow the instructions in the ad. If none are given, a text-based PDF or a .docx file is usually fine. Avoid a scanned PDF: it is just an image.

A prompt to extract keywords with AI

ChatGPT, Claude or a similar assistant can save you time on steps 2 to 5 above. Paste the prompt below, then the job ad and your resume. Always check the result: AI can miss nuance or suggest skills you do not have.

You are an experienced recruiter. I will give you a job ad and my resume.

1. List the 15 most important keywords in the job ad, grouped as: hard skills, tools, soft skills, remote-work skills. Keep the exact wording used in the ad.

2. For each keyword, tell me if it appears in my resume: yes, partly (different wording), or no.

3. For each "partly" or "no", suggest one honest bullet point I could add, based only on what is already in my resume. If my resume gives no evidence, write "no evidence - do not add".

4. Suggest a headline and a 3-line summary that use the top 5 keywords naturally.

Do not invent experience, numbers or tools. Job ad: [paste here]. My resume: [paste here].
