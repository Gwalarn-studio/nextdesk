32 Remote-First Companies Hiring in 2026

A checked list of remote-first employers, where each one says it hires, and a simple method to apply without falling for fake remote jobs.

Updated October 23, 2026
In short
  • We checked each company below on its own careers or culture page in October 2026. Each one says it is remote, remote-first or distributed.
  • "Remote" rarely means "anywhere". Many roles are limited to a country, a region or a range of time zones. Always read the location line of the job ad.
  • Apply on the company's own careers page, with a resume and a short note adapted to the role.
  • Real employers never ask you to pay for a job, a training kit or equipment.

How we built this list

There are many "remote companies" lists online. A lot of them are old. Some companies on them went back to the office years ago. So we used one simple rule: a company is listed only if its own website says it works remotely, and we checked that page in October 2026.

For each company, the link goes to the careers page we checked. The "Where they hire" column only repeats what the company says. When it says nothing precise, we wrote "Not stated". That does not mean "anywhere". It means you must check each job ad.

Two more things to know before you start:

  • Open roles change every week. A few companies on this list showed no open roles when we checked (we say so). They are still worth following.
  • Remote-first is not always fully remote. Some companies below have offices too, or mix remote work with in-person time. We note it when their page says so.

How to read a remote job ad

Before you apply, find these four lines in the ad. They save a lot of time.

  1. Location or eligibility. "Remote, US", "Remote, EMEA" or "Remote, GMT±3" are all restrictions. Companies often limit roles to countries where they can legally employ people.
  2. Time zone or working hours. Some teams need a few hours of overlap each day. Check that you can really work those hours.
  3. Contract type. Employee, contractor, or employed through an employer-of-record service. This changes your taxes, benefits and social security.
  4. Travel. Many remote companies hold team meetups once or twice a year. Make sure you can travel if needed.

How to apply to a remote-first company

1. Apply on the company's own careers page

Job boards are good for discovery. But when you find a role, go to the company's site and apply there. You get the latest version of the ad, and you avoid copied or fake listings. Our guide to the best remote job boards explains how to use both.

2. Show that you can work remotely

Remote employers look for people who write clearly, manage their own time and work well async. Show it with facts: projects you led with people in other countries, tools you use, results you delivered without close supervision. Our remote job resume example shows how to put this on one page.

3. Match the words of the ad

Most of these companies use an applicant tracking system. Use the same words as the ad for your skills and tools, when they are true. See our list of ATS resume keywords.

4. Write a short, specific note

Many forms have a "cover letter" or "why us" field. Keep it short. Say why this company, why this role, and give one proof. Here is a template you can adapt.

Short application note for a remote role

Adapt every line in brackets. Keep it under 150 words.

Opening

Hello [team or hiring manager name], I am applying for the [job title] role. I have used [product] for [time / context], and I like how your team [one specific thing from their careers page or blog].

Proof
  • [Result with a number, e.g. "Cut first-reply time from 9 to 3 hours for a support team of 6"]
  • [Remote proof, e.g. "Worked two years with a team spread across 4 time zones, mostly in writing"]
Practical details

I am based in [country], can work [hours / time zone overlap], and am eligible to work as [employee / contractor] there.

Close

Thank you for reading. I would be glad to talk about how I could help with [one goal from the ad].

How to spot fake remote job scams

Remote jobs attract scammers, because everything happens online. Some even use the names of real companies, like the ones in this table. The US Federal Trade Commission lists the common signs on its job scams page. Here are the main red flags:

  • You are asked to pay. For training, a starter kit, equipment, a certificate or "processing". The FTC is clear: don't pay for the promise of a job.
  • A check arrives before you start. You are told to deposit it, buy equipment and send back the rest. The check later bounces, and you lose the money.
  • The pay is too good for the work. High pay, few skills needed, no experience asked.
  • You were "hired" without a real interview. Only chat messages, often on a messaging app.
  • The email address does not match the company. A recruiter from a known company writes from a free email address, or from a domain that looks almost right.
  • They ask for sensitive data too early. Bank details, ID scans or social security numbers before any signed contract.
  • The job is reshipping packages or moving money for someone. These are never real jobs.

The simplest protection: find the role on the company's own careers page. If the job is not there, contact the company through its official website before you share anything. If you think you were targeted, report the listing to the job board and keep screenshots.

A simple weekly routine

  • Monday: check the careers pages of your 5 to 10 target companies.
  • Tuesday to Thursday: send one or two careful applications a day, each with an adapted resume and note.
  • Friday: update a simple tracker (company, role, date, link, next step) and follow up politely on applications older than two weeks.

Quality beats volume. Ten tailored applications to companies that hire where you live are worth more than a hundred copy-paste ones.
