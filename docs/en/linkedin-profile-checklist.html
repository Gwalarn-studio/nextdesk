LinkedIn Profile Checklist: 15 Points Recruiters Check

An interactive checklist to make your LinkedIn profile clear, findable and convincing in one afternoon.

Updated October 23, 2026
In short
  • Recruiters scan your photo, headline and current role first. Get those three right before anything else.
  • Your headline is your search keyword line. Say what you do and for whom, not just your job title.
  • The About section should read like a short pitch: who you help, how, and proof.
  • Tick the 15 points below. Your progress is saved in this browser, so you can come back later.

A recruiter often opens your LinkedIn profile before your CV. Sometimes instead of it. In a few seconds, they decide whether to keep reading. This checklist covers the 15 points that shape that first impression. Work through it with your profile open in another tab.

1. Your photo and banner: the first two seconds

Your photo appears everywhere: in search results, comments, messages and invitations. A profile without a photo looks unfinished, and many people simply skip it.

You do not need a studio. A phone, a window and a plain wall are enough.

  • Good: head and shoulders, face taking up most of the frame, natural smile, clothes you would wear to an interview in your field.
  • Bad: a cropped vacation group photo, sunglasses, a very dark image, or a logo instead of your face.

The banner is free space. Use it for your field (a code editor, a design board, a city skyline) or a short line like "Data analyst · Remote · EN/FR". Keep it simple. Your photo covers part of it on mobile.

2. The headline: your most important line

The headline is the line under your name. It shows in search results, so it works like a keyword line and a pitch at the same time. You have about 220 characters. You do not need all of them, but "Marketing Manager at X" wastes most of the space.

A simple formula: role + specialty or industry + the value you bring. Add a key tool or "remote" if it matters for the jobs you want.

  • Bad: "Looking for new opportunities" (says nothing about what you do).
  • Bad: "Passionate, motivated, team player" (anyone can write this).
  • Good: a clear role, the words recruiters search for, and one concrete angle.

Here are three headlines you can copy and adapt.

Customer Success Manager | SaaS B2B | I help clients adopt the product and renew | Remote · English & French

Front-end Developer (React, TypeScript) | Fast, accessible web apps | Open to remote roles in Europe

Project Coordinator moving into Operations | Planning, budgets and process improvement | Excel, Notion, Asana

Not sure which keywords to use? Look at five job ads you would apply to and note the words that come back. Our guide on ATS resume keywords explains how to find them. The same words work on LinkedIn.

3. The About section: a short pitch, not a biography

The About section has room for about 2,600 characters. But on most screens only the first two or three lines show before "see more". Those lines must make people want to click.

Write in the first person. Keep paragraphs short. Avoid a block of adjectives. Show what you do, for whom, and give proof.

  • Bad opening: "I am a dynamic and results-driven professional with a strong passion for excellence."
  • Good opening: "I help small e-commerce brands turn their customer data into clear weekly decisions."

Use this template as a starting point. Replace everything in brackets.

I help [type of company or team] [achieve a specific result] through [your main skill or method].

Over the past [number] years as a [role], I have [one concrete achievement with a number or a clear outcome]. Before that, I [second achievement or relevant experience].

What I do best:
• [Skill or task 1]
• [Skill or task 2]
• [Skill or task 3]

Tools I use every day: [tool 1], [tool 2], [tool 3].

I am currently looking for [type of role] in [location / remote], where I can [what you want to contribute]. Feel free to message me or write to [email].

4. Experience: results beat duties

Many profiles copy the job description: "Responsible for managing client accounts." That tells the reader your duties, not your impact. Recruiters want to know what changed because you were there.

Duty (weak)Result (strong)
Managed social media accounts.Ran three brand accounts and doubled monthly engagement in six months.
Handled customer requests.Answered around 40 tickets a day and cut the average response time from 24 to 6 hours.
Worked on the website.Rebuilt the checkout page with the dev team, which reduced abandoned carts.

Use your own real numbers. If you do not have exact figures, describe scope (team size, budget, number of clients) or a clear before/after. Two to four bullets per role are enough. Older roles can be shorter.

Keep this section in line with your CV. If a recruiter sees different job titles or dates, it creates doubt. For a CV that matches a remote-friendly profile, see our remote job resume example.

5. Skills and endorsements

Skills help you appear in recruiter searches. Pick the ones that match your target roles, not everything you have ever touched. Mix hard skills (SQL, Figma, accounting) and a few relevant soft skills (stakeholder management, negotiation).

You can link skills to specific experiences. Do it: it shows where you actually used them. Then pin your top skills so they appear first.

6. Open to Work: visible or private?

The Open to Work feature lets you say which job titles, locations and work types (remote, hybrid, on-site) you want. You can choose between two options:

  • Recruiters only: the information is shared with people using LinkedIn's recruiter tools. Good if you are employed and want discretion. LinkedIn says it tries to hide this from your current company's recruiters, but it cannot fully guarantee it.
  • All LinkedIn members: adds the green frame to your photo. Good if you are not working and want your network to help.

Whatever you choose, be precise with job titles. "Any role" helps nobody find you.

7. Custom URL and contact details

A URL like linkedin.com/in/jane-doe-4b7a91234 looks messy on a CV or email signature. Go to your public profile settings and change it to your name. If it is taken, add your field or a middle initial.

Also check your contact info. If you want to be contacted, add a professional email address there.

8. Recommendations and Featured

Recommendations are proof written by someone else. Two or three specific, recent recommendations are worth more than ten vague ones. Ask a former manager or client and make it easy: remind them of a project you worked on together and the skill you would like them to mention.

The Featured section sits near the top of your profile. Pin your best proof: a portfolio, a case study, a presentation, a post that got good discussion, or a certificate. One or two strong items are enough.

9. Activity: show you are alive

You do not need to become an influencer. But a profile with zero activity for two years can look abandoned. A few simple habits help:

  • Leave a thoughtful comment on a post in your field once or twice a week.
  • Share a short lesson from a project, a tool you like, or a resource you found useful.
  • Congratulate people in your network on new roles. It keeps contacts warm.

Comments are also visible in your activity section, so keep them professional and useful.

A quick final check

  1. Open your profile in a private browser window to see the public view.
  2. Check it on your phone. Most people will see it there.
  3. Read your headline and the first lines of your About aloud. Would a stranger understand what you do?
  4. Compare titles and dates with your CV.
